How can we approach the challenge of invasive alien species in a plural way, without iterating extermination logics, but attending to the multiplicity of relations —sometimes in divergence— that human–animal entanglements irritate? What methods, techniques, and practices can articulate models of coexistence and management that recognize situated life projects and territorial sovereignties?

Wild boar —*Sus scrofa* for biology, *chancho* in the southern Andes— has become one of the most damaging invasive alien species in the Pewenche cordillera. Institutional approaches, however, have tended to prioritize a biosanitary gaze that not only overlooks the complex multispecies relations that mark human–wild boar–mountain coexistence, but also invisibilizes Indigenous and campesino knowledges.

Drawing on the figure of contact zones (Haraway 2023), this project approaches wild boar as an entity constituted in —and facilitating the exchange of— diverse tensions and relations, whether ecological, epistemological, economic, or affective.

The contact-zone figure also helps us assemble a transdisciplinary and intercultural project made not only through collaboration across scientific fields —anthropology, ecology, participatory methods— but also among diverse knowledge systems. The project is articulated in substantive collaboration with the Winkulmapu Association, a grouping of Mapuche communities from Kurarewe that achieved the management of 17,000 hectares in the Puesco-Lanín sector of Villarrica National Park ([See Territory](/en/territorio)). In particular, the project establishes a research alliance with the Wildlife Group of the Winkulmapu Association, articulating a research model that respectfully and territorially entangles diverse knowledge systems ([see Team](/en/equipo)).

## Objetivos

In this framework, the project is structured around four key objectives:

1. **Characterize the ecology of *Sus scrofa*** in the study area —estimated population size, mobility, and diet.
2. **Understand forms of human–wild boar coexistence** —history and everyday cohabitation with *chanchos* in Indigenous and campesino life projects.
3. **Identify local knowledges of management, control, and coexistence with the *chancho*** —including tracking, hunting, and handling techniques and practices.
4. **Co-design community management programs** —multi-actor proposals for the territorial management of *Sus scrofa*.

## Metodología

### Expanding socioecology: methodological intra-action

The project is articulated through three complementary methodological approaches:

**Field ecology**

- Population/density: 60 camera traps and transects.
- Mobility and distribution: thermal camera (drone).
- Diet analysis: fecal samples and hunted individuals (opportunistic).
- Habitability relations: landscape analysis.

**Ethnography (multispecies)**

- Cohabitation narratives: 60 in-depth interviews (neighbors, park rangers, hunters, etc.).
- Practices and technologies: 40 participant observations (hunting, summer pastures, *piñoneo*, etc.).
- Management experience: 30 visits (gardens, crops, meadows, etc.).

**Participatory methods**

- 4 community mappings.
- 2 co-design workshops.
- 2 *trawun*.
- 1 art project.

Thinking with the contact-zone figure, however, our project will not define objectives by method —assigning, as often happens in socioecological research, one method —biological or socio-anthropological— to each objective. Under that conventional model, method integration happens a posteriori: ecological results on one side and socio-anthropological results on the other are brought into dialogue only after each has already been produced with its own methods. By contrast, our methodological architecture integrates biological, socio-anthropological, and participatory methods across all objectives. Following Karen Barad (2007), we call this model “methodological intra-action”: we operationalize research results as the emergent effect of the exchange among different methods and epistemologies within each objective.
