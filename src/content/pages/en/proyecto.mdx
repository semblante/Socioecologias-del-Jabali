## Core question

How can we analyze and understand the socioecological impacts of *Sus scrofa* in the Andean Araucanía from a transdisciplinary, intercultural, and multispecies perspective, in order to propose community-based strategies for invasive species management?

Our leading hypothesis is that invasive alien species are not discrete entities with separable biological, social, ecological, or political effects, but **contact zones** (Haraway, 2003): nodes of relation among entities, knowledges, epistemological systems, and actors in reciprocal exchange.

## Why wild boar, why here

The Andean Araucanía combines world-renowned biodiversity with exceptional intercultural richness. Wild boar cross native forests, summer pastures, *piñoneo*, campesino agriculture, and Mapuche-Pewenche spiritual territories. They do not fit a pest/resource binary: they demand contextualized readings.

The project sits at the intersection of conservation ecology, cultural anthropology, and science and technology studies, and formally integrates Mapuche **kimün** throughout the research cycle.

## Objectives

1. **Ecology of *Sus scrofa*** — population size, mobility, and diet.
2. **Cohabitation** — history and everyday life with wild boar in Indigenous and campesino life projects.
3. **Local knowledge** — tracking, hunting, and management practices.
4. **Community programs** — multi-actor management proposals.

## Hypotheses

1. Understanding wild boar requires co-construction among actors across the full research cycle.
2. Boars are a socioecological phenomenon beyond simplistic good/bad or harm/joy framings.
3. Management must be territorially integrated, with differentiated assessments for agriculture, biodiversity, and Mapuche worlds.

## Method: intra-action

Each objective combines field ecology, ethnography, and participatory methods —camera traps and thermal drones; interviews and participant observation; community mapping, co-design workshops, and *trawun*.

## Case

The core site is the **Puesco-Lanín** sector of Villarrica National Park (17,000 hectares under a Governance Agreement among the Winkulmapu Indigenous Association, CONAF, and the Ministry of National Assets), with secondary cases in Quiñenahuin, Panqui, and Palguín Alto.

## Outputs

Scientific papers, a community book, policy briefs, and an art exhibition with local artists —plus transferable guidelines for Chile’s new conservation policies (SBAP).
